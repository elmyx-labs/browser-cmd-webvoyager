FINAL: Cambridge Dictionary’s English Grammar Today article “Present perfect simple (I have worked)” explains the uses and sentence forms.

Source: https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/present-perfect-simple-i-have-worked

The tense links the past with the present. Cambridge lists these uses:
• Experiences up to now, without specifying an important time.
• Very recent completed events or states, often with just or recently.
• Past actions with present results.
• Situations that began in the past and continue now. For gives a duration (for three years); since gives a starting point (since 2006). How long …? asks about duration.
• Yet, mainly in questions and negative statements, for intended actions that remain undone.
• Already to emphasise completion or achievement, often before the expected time.
• Still to emphasise that something expected continues not to happen.
• Introducing a recent news event in the present perfect, followed by past-simple details. The tense can also introduce a general point, with the past simple supplying specific information about it.

Sentence forms and examples assembled from Cambridge’s form table:
• Affirmative: subject + have/has + past participle — “I have worked.”
• Negative: subject + have/has not + past participle — “I have not worked.”
• Interrogative: Have/Has + subject + past participle? — “Have you worked?”
Use has with he/she/it and have with I/you/we/they. Negative contractions are haven’t and hasn’t.

Cambridge states that American English often uses the past simple instead of the present perfect simple, often with already and yet. Its comparison table contrasts American past-simple questions with British present-perfect questions about eating and finishing.
