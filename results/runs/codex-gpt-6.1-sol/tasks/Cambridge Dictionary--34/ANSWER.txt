FINAL: Cambridge Dictionary’s English Grammar Today explains comparative and superlative adjectives as follows.

COMPARATIVES AND SUPERLATIVES
A comparative compares one person or thing with another: “Maya is taller than Leo.” A superlative identifies the member with the highest or lowest degree of a quality within a whole group: “Maya is the tallest student in her class.” Traditionally, use the comparative for two members; Cambridge notes that a superlative is also used informally for two.

HOW TO FORM THEM
• Most one-syllable adjectives: add -er / -est: small → smaller → smallest.
• Final -e: add -r / -st: nice → nicer → nicest.
• A one-syllable adjective ending in one vowel followed by one consonant doubles its final consonant: big → bigger → biggest; hot → hotter → hottest.
• Two-syllable adjectives ending in -y: change y to i, then add -er / -est: happy → happier → happiest; busy → busier → busiest.
• Some other two-syllable adjectives also take these endings: clever → cleverer → cleverest; quiet → quieter → quietest; narrow → narrower → narrowest; simple → simpler → simplest.
• Two-syllable adjectives ending in -ful normally use more / most, or least: more useful, most useful, least harmful.
• Adjectives with three or more syllables use more / less for the comparative and most / least for the superlative: more interesting, less complicated, most interesting, least complicated.
• Irregular forms include good → better → best; bad → worse → worst; far → farther/further → farthest/furthest. Cambridge also lists older/elder and oldest/eldest for old.

Example sentences: “This room is bigger than the kitchen.” “Monday was our busiest day.” “Her explanation is more useful than mine.” “This is the least expensive option.”

HOW TO USE THEM
• Use than to introduce the second comparison term: “This train is faster than the bus.” With a personal pronoun, an object form is usual: “She is taller than me.” A formal alternative is a subject pronoun plus be: “She is taller than I am.”
• Strengthen a comparative with much, a lot, far, even or rather; soften it with a little or a bit: “The new route is much shorter.” “This chair is a bit more comfortable.” Than ever can also emphasise a comparison.
• Show continuing change with -er and -er or more and more, without than: “The nights are getting colder and colder.” “The work is becoming more and more difficult.”
• Show linked changes with the + comparative …, the + comparative …: “The warmer the room is, the sleepier I feel.”
• Less normally accompanies longer adjectives. With a short adjective, prefer not as … as (or not so … as): “This exercise is less complicated than the last one.” “This bag is not as heavy as mine.”
• Normally put the before a superlative followed by a noun: “It is the oldest house in the village.” Possessives can replace the: “That was my best result.” Numbers are also possible: “She is the second youngest child.”
• In informal speech, the can be omitted after a linking or sense verb when no noun follows: “Which tastes best?”
• Use in before a singular place or group, and of before a plural group: “She is the youngest player in the team.” “She is the youngest of the three.”
• Emphasise a superlative with by far, easily or of all: “This is by far the easiest route.” Cambridge also allows quite in formal usage.
• A to-infinitive can follow a superlative: “She was the youngest runner to finish.” Comparison clauses can be shortened: “The journey was longer than expected.”

COMMON ERRORS
Do not combine more/most with an -er/-est comparative or superlative: use better, not more better; biggest, not most biggest. Use than, rather than that or as, to introduce the second term after a comparative. When comparing a member with an entire group of more than two, use the superlative.
